## What you will learn

- the terms of electrolysis and the movement of ions;
- products at each electrode for molten and aqueous electrolytes;
- quantitative electrolysis (Faraday).

## 1. Key terms

**Electrolysis** uses electricity to decompose a compound that is **molten or dissolved** (so the ions are free to move). **Electrolyte**: the substance decomposed. **Electrodes**: the conductors (inert graphite/platinum or active metal).

- **Cathode** (negative): **reduction**; cations move toward it.
- **Anode** (positive): **oxidation**; anions move toward it.

Mnemonics: **PANIC** — Positive Anode, Negative Is Cathode; **RED CAT, AN OX**.

## 2. Molten compounds

The metal forms at the cathode and the non-metal at the anode: molten $\text{NaCl} \to \text{Na}$ (cathode), $\text{Cl}_2$ (anode); molten $\text{PbBr}_2 \to \text{Pb}$ and $\text{Br}_2$. *Why must lead bromide be molten?* In the solid the ions are fixed and cannot carry current.

## 3. Aqueous solutions

Water also supplies $\text{H}^+$ and $\text{OH}^-$.

- At the **cathode**: the less reactive of the metal and hydrogen is discharged ($\text{Cu}^{2+} \to \text{Cu}$; but $\text{Na}^+$ gives $\text{H}_2$).
- At the **anode**: a halide is discharged in preference to hydroxide ($\text{Cl}_2$); otherwise $\text{OH}^-$ gives $\text{O}_2$: $4\text{OH}^- \to 2\text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{O}_2 + 4e^-$.

| Electrolyte | Cathode | Anode |
|---|---|---|
| Molten NaCl | Na | $\text{Cl}_2$ |
| Concentrated NaCl(aq) | $\text{H}_2$ (NaOH left in solution) | $\text{Cl}_2$ |
| $\text{CuSO}_4(aq)$, inert electrodes | Cu | $\text{O}_2$ |
| Dilute $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ | $\text{H}_2$ | $\text{O}_2$ ($2 : 1$ by volume) |
| KI(aq) | $\text{H}_2$ | $\text{I}_2$ |

**Active electrodes**: with copper electrodes in $\text{CuSO}_4$ the anode dissolves and copper plates onto the cathode: used to **refine copper** and to **electroplate** (the object to be plated is the cathode).

## 4. Quantitative

$Q = It$; moles of electrons $= \dfrac Q{96\,500}$; amount of metal $= \dfrac{n_{e^-}}z$.

**Example 1.** $2\ \text{A}$ for $965\ \text{s}$: $Q = 1930\ \text{C}$, $n_{e^-} = 0.02$; Cu deposited $0.01\ \text{mol} = 0.64\ \text{g}$.

**Example 2.** Silver-plating a spoon: spoon = cathode, silver = anode, electrolyte $\text{AgNO}_3$: $\text{Ag}^+ + e^- \to \text{Ag}$; for $2\ \text{A}$ over $965\ \text{s}$: $m = 0.02 \cdot 108 = 2.16\ \text{g}$.

**Example 3.** Electrolysis of dilute $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ gives hydrogen and oxygen in the ratio $2 : 1$ (it is really electrolysis of water).

## Common mistakes

- Swapping the cathode and the anode.
- Forgetting that water takes part in aqueous electrolysis.
- Treating the electrolytic cell like a galvanic cell (the anode is *positive* here).

## Practice problems

1. Products when molten aluminium oxide is electrolysed.
2. Products when aqueous KI is electrolysed with inert electrodes.

**Answers.** (1) Aluminium at the cathode and oxygen at the anode. (2) Hydrogen at the cathode, iodine at the anode (KOH left).
