## What you will learn

- percentage yield and atom economy, and the difference between them;
- calculations and comparison of routes;
- the principles of green chemistry.

## 1. Yield

The **theoretical yield** is the maximum mass of product calculated from the limiting reagent; the **actual yield** is what is obtained.

$$
\text{percentage yield} = \frac{\text{actual yield}}{\text{theoretical yield}} \times 100\%.
$$

**Example 1.** A student expected $10\ \text{g}$ and obtained $7.4\ \text{g}$: yield $74\%$. Reasons for a low yield: incomplete reaction (equilibrium), side reactions, loss in transfer, filtration and crystallisation, impure product.

**Example 2.** Calculate the theoretical mass of $\text{MgO}$ from $4.8\ \text{g}$ of Mg: $0.2\ \text{mol}$, $8.0\ \text{g}$; actual $7.2\ \text{g}$: $90\%$.

## 2. Atom economy

$$
\text{atom economy} = \frac{M_r(\text{desired product})}{\sum M_r(\text{reactants})} \times 100\%.
$$

It measures the proportion of the reactant mass that ends up in the useful product, **independently of the practical yield**. **Addition and combination** reactions can have $100\%$ atom economy; **substitution, elimination and decomposition** produce by-products.

**Example 3.** $\text{C} + \text{H}_2\text{O} \to \text{CO} + \text{H}_2$ with $\text{H}_2$ wanted: $\dfrac2{30} = 6.7\%$.

**Example 4.** $\text{CaCO}_3 \to \text{CaO} + \text{CO}_2$ with CaO wanted: $\dfrac{56}{100} = 56\%$.

**Example 5 (comparing routes).** Ethanol by hydration of ethene: $\text{C}_2\text{H}_4 + \text{H}_2\text{O} \to \text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH}$ ($100\%$); by substitution $\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{Br} + \text{NaOH} \to \text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH} + \text{NaBr}$ ($\dfrac{46}{149} = 31\%$): the hydration is greener.

**Example 6.** Iron: $\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3 + 3\text{CO} \to 2\text{Fe} + 3\text{CO}_2$: $\dfrac{112}{160 + 84} = 45.9\%$.

**Important**: do not confuse yield with atom economy, and do not divide by the mass of only one reactant.

## 3. Green chemistry (绿色化学)

Principles:

1. **Prevent pollution at its source** rather than clean it up.
2. Design reactions with **high atom economy**.
3. Use less hazardous reagents and **safer solvents** (water, supercritical $\text{CO}_2$).
4. Design **energy-efficient** processes (catalysts, ambient temperature and pressure).
5. Use **renewable feedstocks** (bio-ethanol, plant oils).
6. **Recycle** unreacted reagents and by-products (as in the Haber and Contact processes).
7. Design products that **degrade** after use.
8. Monitor and prevent accidents.

**Example 7.** Why is the Haber process "green" in this sense? Unreacted gases are recycled and atom economy is $100\%$; but it uses large energy and a fossil-fuel hydrogen source.

## Common mistakes

- Confusing yield with atom economy.
- Using the wrong $M_r$ values (include all reactants).
- Forgetting the state of the question: theoretical yield is from the limiting reagent.

## Practice problems

1. Atom economy for $\text{N}_2 + 3\text{H}_2 \to 2\text{NH}_3$.
2. Percentage yield when $5.6\ \text{g}$ is obtained from a theoretical $8.0\ \text{g}$.

**Answers.** (1) $100\%$. (2) $70\%$.
