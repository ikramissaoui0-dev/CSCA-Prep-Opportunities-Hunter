## What you will learn

- the stages and conditions of the Contact process for sulfuric acid;
- the chlor-alkali process and its products;
- typical exam questions on both.

## 1. The Contact process

1. **Making $\text{SO}_2$**: $\text{S} + \text{O}_2 \to \text{SO}_2$ (or roasting sulfide ores).
2. **Catalytic oxidation**: $2\text{SO}_2 + \text{O}_2 \rightleftharpoons 2\text{SO}_3$, $\Delta H = -197\ \text{kJ/mol}$; **$\text{V}_2\text{O}_5$ catalyst, $450\ ^\circ\text{C}$, $1$–$2\ \text{atm}$**.
3. **Absorption**: $\text{SO}_3$ is absorbed in $98\%$ sulfuric acid to form oleum ($\text{H}_2\text{S}_2\text{O}_7$), then diluted: $\text{H}_2\text{S}_2\text{O}_7 + \text{H}_2\text{O} \to 2\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$. Absorbing $\text{SO}_3$ directly in water would give a fine acid mist (the reaction is violently exothermic).

**Reasons for the conditions.** The reaction is exothermic, so a low temperature favours the product, but is too slow even with the catalyst: $450\ ^\circ\text{C}$ is a compromise. The equilibrium conversion is already about $99\%$ at atmospheric pressure, so **high pressure is not worth the cost** (it adds corrosion and equipment cost). **Excess air** (cheap oxygen) pushes the equilibrium forward and ensures that the valuable $\text{SO}_2$ is converted. Heat exchangers and several catalyst beds with cooling in between keep the temperature in the best range.

Uses of sulfuric acid: fertilisers (superphosphate), detergents, paints, batteries, chemical manufacture.

**Example 1.** *Why is excess air used in the converter?* To shift the equilibrium toward $\text{SO}_3$ and make sure nearly all the $\text{SO}_2$ is oxidised; oxygen is cheap.

## 2. The chlor-alkali industry

Electrolysis of concentrated brine ($\text{NaCl(aq)}$) in a **membrane cell**:

- **Anode**: $2\text{Cl}^- \to \text{Cl}_2 + 2e^-$.
- **Cathode**: $2\text{H}_2\text{O} + 2e^- \to \text{H}_2 + 2\text{OH}^-$.
- Left in solution: $\text{Na}^+$ and $\text{OH}^-$, i.e. **NaOH**.

$$
2\text{NaCl} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} \xrightarrow{\text{electrolysis}} 2\text{NaOH} + \text{H}_2 + \text{Cl}_2.
$$

Products in the ratio $\text{NaOH} : \text{H}_2 : \text{Cl}_2 = 2 : 1 : 1$. A **cation-exchange membrane** allows only $\text{Na}^+$ to pass, so that $\text{Cl}_2$ and NaOH are kept apart (otherwise they react to form bleach) and the NaOH is purer than from the diaphragm cell; the mercury cell was abandoned for pollution reasons.

**Uses**: $\text{Cl}_2$ (water treatment, PVC, bleach, solvents); $\text{H}_2$ (fuel, ammonia); NaOH (soap, paper, aluminium, textiles).

**Example 2.** In a cell diagram, the electrode connected to the **negative** terminal of the supply is the cathode.

**Example 3.** $4\ \text{t}$ of NaOH ($10^5\ \text{mol}$) comes with $0.5 \times 10^5\ \text{mol}$ of $\text{Cl}_2$, i.e. $3.55\ \text{t}$; producing $1\ \text{mol}$ of $\text{Cl}_2$ needs $2\ \text{mol}$ of electrons ($193\,000\ \text{C}$).

## 3. Environmental notes

Sulfur dioxide emissions are limited by the double-absorption method and by scrubbing; chlorine is toxic and must be handled safely; mercury cells were phased out.

## Common mistakes

- Giving the same conditions for the Haber and Contact processes.
- Forgetting that NaOH remains in the solution during brine electrolysis.
- Saying $\text{SO}_3$ is absorbed directly in water.

## Practice problems

1. Overall equation for the chlor-alkali process.
2. Why is the Contact process run at about $1$–$2\ \text{atm}$?

**Answers.** (1) $2\text{NaCl} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} \to 2\text{NaOH} + \text{H}_2 + \text{Cl}_2$. (2) The yield is already about $99\%$, so a high pressure only adds cost and safety risk.
