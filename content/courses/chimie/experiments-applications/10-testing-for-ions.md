## What you will learn

- the tests for common cations and anions;
- why the order and acidification matter;
- how to identify an unknown salt.

## 1. Cations

Add NaOH solution, a few drops then excess:

| Ion | Result |
|---|---|
| $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ | pale-blue precipitate, insoluble in excess |
| $\text{Fe}^{2+}$ | green precipitate, turning red-brown in air |
| $\text{Fe}^{3+}$ | red-brown precipitate |
| $\text{Al}^{3+}$, $\text{Zn}^{2+}$, $\text{Pb}^{2+}$ | white precipitate, **dissolves in excess** |
| $\text{Mg}^{2+}$, $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ | white precipitate, insoluble in excess |
| $\text{NH}_4^+$ | warm with NaOH: $\text{NH}_3$ (damp red litmus turns blue) |

Aqueous ammonia: $\text{Zn}^{2+}$ redissolves in excess, $\text{Al}^{3+}$ does not. **Confirmatory tests**: $\text{Fe}^{3+}$ + $\text{SCN}^-$ (blood red); $\text{Fe}^{2+}$ + hexacyanoferrate(III) (blue). **Flame tests**: Li red, Na yellow, K lilac, Ca orange-red, Cu blue-green.

## 2. Anions

| Ion | Test |
|---|---|
| $\text{CO}_3^{2-}$ | add dilute acid: $\text{CO}_2$ turns limewater milky |
| $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$ | dilute HCl, then $\text{BaCl}_2$: white $\text{BaSO}_4$ |
| $\text{Cl}^-$, $\text{Br}^-$, $\text{I}^-$ | dilute $\text{HNO}_3$, then $\text{AgNO}_3$: white, cream, yellow precipitates |
| $\text{NO}_3^-$ | warm with NaOH and aluminium foil: $\text{NH}_3$ |
| $\text{OH}^-$ | turns red litmus blue |

**Always acidify first** with the right acid (HCl for sulfate, $\text{HNO}_3$ for halide): it removes carbonate, which would give white $\text{BaCO}_3$ or $\text{Ag}_2\text{CO}_3$ and mislead you. Test for carbonate before halide.

## 3. Worked questions

**Example 1.** A student adds $\text{BaCl}_2$ to a solution; a white precipitate forms; concluding it contains sulfate is **not** justified (carbonate, or $\text{Ag}^+$, also gives a white precipitate). Add dilute HCl first: if the precipitate remains it is $\text{BaSO}_4$ (provided no $\text{Ag}^+$ is present).

**Example 2.** Show that a pale-green solution contains $\text{Fe}^{2+}$: add NaOH (a green precipitate that turns brown), or add KSCN (no colour) then a few drops of chlorine water/hydrogen peroxide (blood red).

**Example 3.** A white solid gives a yellow flame; its solution gives a white precipitate with $\text{AgNO}_3$ (after $\text{HNO}_3$) that dissolves in dilute ammonia: sodium chloride.

**Example 4.** A white precipitate with NaOH that dissolves in excess NaOH and, with excess ammonia, stays: $\text{Al}^{3+}$ (with $\text{Zn}^{2+}$ it would redissolve in ammonia).

**Example 5.** An unknown solid fizzes with acid; the gas turns limewater milky; the solution gives a lilac flame: potassium carbonate.

## Common mistakes

- Confusing $\text{Al}^{3+}$, $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ and $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ (check excess NaOH).
- Forgetting to acidify before the silver or barium test.
- Reporting only the name of the test, not the observation.

## Practice problems

1. Test to distinguish $\text{Fe}^{2+}$ from $\text{Fe}^{3+}$.
2. Test to confirm the presence of ammonium ions.

**Answers.** (1) NaOH: green vs red-brown precipitate. (2) Warm with NaOH; $\text{NH}_3$ turns damp red litmus blue.
