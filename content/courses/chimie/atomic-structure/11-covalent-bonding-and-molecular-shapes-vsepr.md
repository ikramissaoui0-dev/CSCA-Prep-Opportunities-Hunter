## What you will learn

- single, double and triple covalent bonds and dot-and-cross diagrams;
- the VSEPR method for predicting shapes and bond angles;
- polarity, intermolecular forces and simple versus giant molecules.

## 1. Covalent bonding

Atoms **share** pairs of electrons to complete their outer shells. A single bond is one shared pair, a double bond two, a triple bond three (shorter and stronger). A **dative (coordinate) bond** has both shared electrons from one atom ($\text{NH}_4^+$, $\text{H}_3\text{O}^+$).

Dot-and-cross examples: $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ (O shares one pair with each H and keeps two lone pairs); $\text{CO}_2$ ($\text{O=C=O}$, two double bonds); $\text{N}_2$ (a triple bond and a lone pair on each N); $\text{CH}_4$ (four single bonds).

**Properties of simple molecular substances**: low melting and boiling points (only the weak **intermolecular forces** are overcome, not the covalent bonds), no electrical conduction. *Methane boils at $-162\ ^\circ\text{C}$ because of weak London forces between small molecules.* Say "the intermolecular forces are weak", **not** "the bonds are weak".

## 2. VSEPR in three steps

**V**alence **S**hell **E**lectron **P**air **R**epulsion: pairs of electrons around a central atom repel and arrange themselves to be as far apart as possible; lone pairs repel more than bonding pairs.

1. Count the valence electrons of the central atom (adjust for charge).
2. Find the number of electron pairs (bonding + lone).
3. The pairs fix the **electron-pair geometry**; removing the lone pairs gives the **molecular shape**.

| Pairs | Lone pairs | Shape | Angle | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 0 | linear | $180^\circ$ | $\text{CO}_2$, $\text{BeCl}_2$ |
| 3 | 0 | trigonal planar | $120^\circ$ | $\text{BF}_3$, $\text{CO}_3^{2-}$ |
| 3 | 1 | bent | $\approx 119^\circ$ | $\text{SO}_2$ |
| 4 | 0 | tetrahedral | $109.5^\circ$ | $\text{CH}_4$ |
| 4 | 1 | trigonal pyramidal | $\approx 107^\circ$ | $\text{NH}_3$, $\text{SO}_3^{2-}$ |
| 4 | 2 | bent | $\approx 104.5^\circ$ | $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ |
| 5 | 0 | trigonal bipyramidal | $90^\circ$, $120^\circ$ | $\text{PCl}_5$ |
| 6 | 0 | octahedral | $90^\circ$ | $\text{SF}_6$ |

**Example 1.** $\text{NH}_3$: $4$ pairs, $1$ lone pair: pyramidal, $107^\circ$ (the lone pair squeezes the angle below $109.5^\circ$).

**Example 2.** $\text{BeCl}_2$ and $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ both have two bonds, but Be has no lone pair (linear) while O has two (bent).

## 3. Polarity

A bond is **polar** if the atoms differ in electronegativity. A molecule is polar only if the bond dipoles do **not** cancel: $\text{CO}_2$, $\text{CH}_4$, $\text{BF}_3$, $\text{CCl}_4$ are non-polar (symmetric); $\text{H}_2\text{O}$, $\text{NH}_3$, $\text{HCl}$ are polar.

## 4. Intermolecular forces

In increasing strength: **London (dispersion)** forces (all molecules; stronger with more electrons), **dipole–dipole** forces (polar molecules), **hydrogen bonds** (H bonded to N, O or F, attracted to a lone pair on another N, O or F). Hydrogen bonding explains the high boiling point of water ($\text{H}_2\text{S}$ boils at $-60\ ^\circ\text{C}$), the lower density of ice and the properties of DNA. "Like dissolves like": polar substances dissolve in polar solvents.

## 5. Giant covalent structures

Diamond (four bonds per carbon, hard, insulator), graphite (layers, delocalised electrons, conducts, soft), silicon dioxide (high melting point), graphene. See the next module for details.

## Common mistakes

- Saying covalent bonds break when a molecular substance melts.
- Calling a molecule polar just because it has polar bonds.
- Forgetting that lone pairs affect the shape.

## Practice problems

1. Shape and polarity of $\text{PH}_3$ and $\text{CCl}_4$.
2. Predict the shape of $\text{CO}_3^{2-}$.

**Answers.** (1) $\text{PH}_3$ pyramidal, slightly polar; $\text{CCl}_4$ tetrahedral, non-polar. (2) Trigonal planar, $120^\circ$.
