## What you will learn

- how ions form;
- ionic bonding and giant ionic lattices;
- formulae of ionic compounds and the properties of ionic substances.

## 1. Ion formation

Atoms gain or lose electrons to obtain a full outer shell:

- **Metals** lose electrons to form positive **cations**: $\text{Na} \to \text{Na}^+ + e^-$; $\text{Mg} \to \text{Mg}^{2+} + 2e^-$; $\text{Al} \to \text{Al}^{3+} + 3e^-$.
- **Non-metals** gain electrons to form negative **anions**: $\text{Cl} + e^- \to \text{Cl}^-$; $\text{O} + 2e^- \to \text{O}^{2-}$.

The charge of a simple ion is predicted from the group: Group 1 $+1$, Group 2 $+2$, Group 13 $+3$, Group 15 $-3$, Group 16 $-2$, Group 17 $-1$. **Polyatomic ions**: $\text{NH}_4^+$, $\text{OH}^-$, $\text{NO}_3^-$, $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$, $\text{CO}_3^{2-}$, $\text{PO}_4^{3-}$.

## 2. Ionic bonding

**Ionic bonding** (离子键) is the strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions, formed when a metal transfers electrons to a non-metal. The ions pack into a **giant ionic lattice**: each ion is surrounded by ions of opposite charge (in NaCl each ion has $6$ nearest neighbours).

**Dot-and-cross diagram for MgO**: Mg (2 outer electrons) gives both to O (6 outer electrons); show $[\text{Mg}]^{2+}$ and $[\text{O}]^{2-}$ in square brackets, each with a full outer shell of $8$ electrons (the Mg ion shows none).

## 3. Formulae

The compound is electrically neutral: balance the total positive and negative charges. Cross over the charges:

- $\text{Na}^+ + \text{Cl}^- \to \text{NaCl}$; $\text{Ca}^{2+} + 2\text{Cl}^- \to \text{CaCl}_2$ (calcium gives one electron to each of two chlorine atoms);
- $\text{Al}^{3+} + \text{O}^{2-} \to \text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$; $\text{Al}^{3+} + 3\text{OH}^- \to \text{Al(OH)}_3$; $\text{Fe}^{3+} + \text{SO}_4^{2-} \to \text{Fe}_2(\text{SO}_4)_3$.

## 4. Properties

- **High melting and boiling points** (many strong ionic bonds must be broken): $\text{NaCl}$ $801\ ^\circ\text{C}$; $\text{MgO}$ $2852\ ^\circ\text{C}$ (higher charges and smaller ions give stronger attraction).
- **Hard but brittle**: a blow shifts layers so that like charges meet and repel.
- **Conduct electricity when molten or dissolved** (the ions are free to move), **not as solids** (ions fixed in the lattice).
- Many are **soluble in water**.

**Example 1.** *Why does NaCl conduct when dissolved but not as a solid?* In solution the lattice breaks into free ions that carry charge; in the solid the ions cannot move.

**Example 2.** *Why is the formula of calcium chloride $\text{CaCl}_2$?* Calcium forms $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ and chlorine $\text{Cl}^-$, so two chloride ions balance one calcium ion.

**Example 3.** Describe the formation of MgO: each Mg atom loses two electrons to become $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ and each O atom gains two to become $\text{O}^{2-}$; both then have a full outer shell, and the ions attract.

## Common mistakes

- Showing electrons shared rather than transferred in an ionic diagram.
- Forgetting the brackets and charges.
- Writing $\text{MgCl}$ instead of $\text{MgCl}_2$.

## Practice problems

1. Formula of aluminium oxide and of calcium nitrate.
2. Why is MgO's melting point higher than NaCl's?

**Answers.** (1) $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$; $\text{Ca(NO}_3)_2$. (2) Higher ionic charges ($2+$, $2-$) and smaller ions give a stronger lattice.
