## What you will learn

- the structure of the atom and its subatomic particles;
- atomic number, mass number and the number of neutrons;
- atoms, ions and how to read nuclide notation.

## 1. Structure of the atom

An atom has a tiny, dense **nucleus** (protons and neutrons) surrounded by **electrons** in shells. The atom is mostly empty space: about $10^{-10}\ \text{m}$ across, with a nucleus about $10^{-15}\ \text{m}$ across.

| Particle | Relative mass | Relative charge | Location |
|---|---|---|---|
| Proton ($p^+$) | $1$ | $+1$ | nucleus |
| Neutron ($n^0$) | $1$ | $0$ | nucleus |
| Electron ($e^-$) | $\approx \tfrac1{1840}$ | $-1$ | shells around the nucleus |

A neutral atom has equal numbers of protons and electrons.

## 2. Key numbers

- **Atomic number** $Z$ = number of protons. It defines the element.
- **Mass number** $A$ = protons + neutrons.
- **Neutrons** $N = A - Z$.

Nuclide notation: ${}^A_ZX$. Ions: a **cation** has lost electrons (positive charge), an **anion** has gained electrons (negative charge); the number of protons does not change.

**Example 1.** Sodium: $Z = 11$, $A = 23$: $11$ protons, $12$ neutrons, $11$ electrons.

**Example 2.** The chloride ion ${}^{35}_{17}\text{Cl}^-$: $17$ protons, $18$ neutrons, $18$ electrons.

**Example 3.** An atom with $26$ protons and $30$ neutrons: $Z = 26$ (iron), $A = 56$.

**Example 4.** ${}^{27}_{13}\text{Al}^{3+}$: $13$ protons, $14$ neutrons, $10$ electrons.

**Example 5.** A particle with $10$ electrons and $11$ protons is $\text{Na}^+$.

## 3. Models of the atom (history)

Dalton (indivisible atoms) $\to$ Thomson (electron discovered; plum pudding) $\to$ Rutherford (nuclear atom, from $\alpha$ scattering) $\to$ Bohr (energy levels) $\to$ quantum model (orbitals as regions of probability).

## 4. Why the electrons matter

Chemical properties depend on the electrons, especially the outer (valence) ones: atoms react to obtain a full outer shell by losing, gaining or sharing electrons. Nuclear properties depend on the nucleus.

## Common mistakes

- Confusing mass number and atomic number.
- Forgetting to subtract electrons for a cation (or add for an anion).
- Changing the number of protons when forming an ion.

## Practice problems

1. Particles in ${}^{19}_9\text{F}$.
2. Particles in ${}^{24}_{12}\text{Mg}^{2+}$.
3. Which particle has almost no mass?

**Answers.** (1) $9\ p$, $10\ n$, $9\ e$. (2) $12\ p$, $12\ n$, $10\ e$. (3) The electron.
