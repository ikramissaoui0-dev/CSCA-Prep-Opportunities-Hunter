## What you will learn

- how covalent bonds form and the types of bond;
- dot-and-cross diagrams and dative bonds;
- why simple molecular substances have low melting points, and how to write model answers.

## 1. Formation

A **covalent bond** is the strong electrostatic attraction between the nuclei of two atoms and a **shared pair of electrons**. It forms between non-metal atoms, each obtaining a full outer shell. A **single bond** is one shared pair, a **double bond** two, a **triple bond** three (shorter and stronger). A **dative (coordinate) bond** has both electrons from one atom: $\text{NH}_4^+$, $\text{H}_3\text{O}^+$, $\text{CO}$.

**Dot-and-cross diagrams** show only the outer electrons: $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ (O shares one pair with each H, two lone pairs on O); $\text{CO}_2$ ($\text{O=C=O}$); $\text{N}_2$ (triple bond, one lone pair on each N); $\text{CH}_4$; $\text{NH}_3$ (one lone pair).

**Bond properties**: bond length decreases and bond strength increases single $\to$ double $\to$ triple; polarity depends on the electronegativity difference.

## 2. Simple molecular substances

Small molecules held by weak **intermolecular forces** (London, dipole–dipole, hydrogen bonds). Properties: **low melting and boiling points** (little energy is needed to overcome the intermolecular forces; the covalent bonds do not break), **do not conduct electricity** (no free ions or electrons), often soluble in non-polar solvents. Melting point rises with molecular size (more electrons, stronger London forces).

**Model answers.**

- *Why does methane boil at $-162\ ^\circ\text{C}$?* It is a small molecule with weak London forces between molecules; little energy is needed to separate them.
- *Why does $\text{CO}_2$ not conduct electricity?* It has no free electrons or ions: all outer electrons are in covalent bonds.
- **Common error**: saying "the bonds are weak". The bonds *within* molecules are strong; it is the forces *between* molecules that are weak.

## 3. Hydrogen bonding

A hydrogen bond forms between an H bonded to N, O or F and a lone pair on another N, O or F. It explains the high boiling point of water compared with $\text{H}_2\text{S}$, the lower density of ice, and the structure of DNA and proteins.

**Example 1.** Draw $\text{H}_2\text{O}$: bond angle $104.5^\circ$ (bent), polar.

**Example 2.** $\text{HF}$ (boiling point $20\ ^\circ\text{C}$) is higher than $\text{HCl}$ ($-85\ ^\circ\text{C}$) because of hydrogen bonding.

**Example 3.** Iodine is a solid and chlorine a gas at room temperature: iodine's larger molecules have stronger London forces.

## Common mistakes

- Saying covalent bonds break when a molecular substance melts.
- Confusing a molecule with a giant structure.
- Forgetting lone pairs in diagrams.

## Practice problems

1. Number of shared pairs in $\text{N}_2$ and $\text{CO}_2$.
2. Why does water have a higher boiling point than $\text{H}_2\text{S}$?

**Answers.** (1) $3$ and $4$ (two double bonds). (2) Hydrogen bonding between water molecules.
