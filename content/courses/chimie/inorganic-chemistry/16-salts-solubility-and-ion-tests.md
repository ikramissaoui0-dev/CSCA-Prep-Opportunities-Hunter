## What you will learn

- solubility rules and how to prepare salts;
- the solubility product $K_{sp}$ and precipitation;
- hydrates and ion test procedures.

## 1. Solubility rules

- All nitrates, and all Group 1 and ammonium salts, are soluble.
- Chlorides are soluble except $\text{AgCl}$ and $\text{PbCl}_2$.
- Sulfates are soluble except $\text{BaSO}_4$, $\text{PbSO}_4$ (and slightly $\text{CaSO}_4$).
- Carbonates, hydroxides and sulfides are mostly insoluble (except Group 1 and ammonium).

## 2. Making salts

- **Soluble salt from an insoluble base, carbonate or metal**: add the solid to warm acid until in excess, filter off the excess, evaporate part of the solvent, cool to crystallise, filter and dry. *Pure dry $\text{CuSO}_4\cdot5\text{H}_2\text{O}$: add excess CuO to warm dilute $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$.*
- **Soluble salt from an alkali**: titrate with an indicator to find the volumes, then repeat without the indicator.
- **Insoluble salt**: mix two solutions (precipitation), filter, wash with distilled water, dry.

## 3. Hydrates

A hydrated salt contains **water of crystallisation**: $\text{CuSO}_4\cdot5\text{H}_2\text{O}$ (blue) loses water on heating to give white anhydrous $\text{CuSO}_4$; adding water turns it blue again (a test for water). $x = \dfrac{n(\text{H}_2\text{O})}{n(\text{salt})}$.

## 4. Solubility product

For a sparingly soluble salt $\text{M}_a\text{X}_b$: $K_{sp} = [\text{M}]^a[\text{X}]^b$ at saturation.

**Example 1.** $\text{AgCl}$ ($K_{sp} = 1.8 \times 10^{-10}$): $s = \sqrt{K_{sp}} = 1.3 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{mol/L}$. $\text{BaSO}_4$ ($K_{sp} = 1.1 \times 10^{-10}$): $s = 1.05 \times 10^{-5}$.

**Precipitation** occurs when the ion product $Q > K_{sp}$. **Example 2.** Mixing equal volumes of $2 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{mol/L}$ $\text{Ba}^{2+}$ and $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$: after mixing $[\text{Ba}^{2+}] = [\text{SO}_4^{2-}] = 10^{-4}$, $Q = 10^{-8} > K_{sp}$: a precipitate forms.

The **common-ion effect** lowers solubility: $\text{AgCl}$ is less soluble in NaCl solution.

## 5. Ion tests (summary)

$\text{Cu}^{2+}$: pale-blue precipitate with NaOH. $\text{Fe}^{2+}$: green, turning brown; $\text{Fe}^{3+}$: red-brown; $\text{Al}^{3+}$, $\text{Zn}^{2+}$: white precipitate soluble in excess NaOH; $\text{NH}_4^+$: $\text{NH}_3$ on warming. $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$: HCl then $\text{BaCl}_2$; $\text{Cl}^-$: $\text{HNO}_3$ then $\text{AgNO}_3$; $\text{CO}_3^{2-}$: acid, limewater. **Always acidify first.**

**Example 3.** A solution gives a white precipitate with NaOH that dissolves in excess NaOH: $\text{Al}^{3+}$, $\text{Zn}^{2+}$ or $\text{Pb}^{2+}$.

**Example 4.** A white precipitate with $\text{BaCl}_2$ does not alone prove sulfate (carbonate also gives one): add dilute HCl first; if the precipitate survives, it is $\text{BaSO}_4$.

## Common mistakes

- Forgetting to acidify before adding $\text{BaCl}_2$ or $\text{AgNO}_3$.
- Confusing $Q$ and $K_{sp}$.
- Not drying the product to constant mass.

## Practice problems

1. Solubility (mol/L) of $\text{AgCl}$ with $K_{sp} = 1.8 \times 10^{-10}$.
2. How would you make pure dry crystals of copper(II) sulfate?

**Answers.** (1) $1.3 \times 10^{-5}$. (2) Add excess CuO to warm dilute $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$, filter, evaporate part of the water, crystallise, filter and dry.
