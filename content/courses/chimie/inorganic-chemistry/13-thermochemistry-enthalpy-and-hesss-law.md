## What you will learn

- enthalpy change and its sign conventions;
- calorimetry, bond energies and standard enthalpies;
- Hess's law and its applications.

## 1. Enthalpy change

The **enthalpy change** $\Delta H$ is the heat change at constant pressure. **Exothermic**: $\Delta H < 0$ (heat released; temperature of the surroundings rises). **Endothermic**: $\Delta H > 0$. Standard conditions: $298\ \text{K}$, $100\ \text{kPa}$.

Definitions: **enthalpy of formation** $\Delta H_f^\circ$ (one mole of a compound from its elements in their standard states; zero for an element); **enthalpy of combustion** $\Delta H_c^\circ$ (one mole burned completely in oxygen); **enthalpy of neutralisation** (one mole of water formed; about $-57\ \text{kJ/mol}$ for a strong acid and a strong base).

## 2. Calorimetry

$Q = mc\Delta T$ (for solutions, $m$ is the mass of the solution, $c = 4.18\ \text{J/g}\,^\circ\text{C}$), and $\Delta H = -\dfrac Qn$ per mole of limiting reagent.

**Example 1.** $50\ \text{mL}$ of $1.0\ \text{mol/L}$ HCl and $50\ \text{mL}$ of $1.0\ \text{mol/L}$ NaOH rise by $6.8\ ^\circ\text{C}$: $Q = 100 \cdot 4.18 \cdot 6.8 = 2842\ \text{J}$; $n = 0.050$; $\Delta H = -56.8\ \text{kJ/mol}$.

*Why is a measured enthalpy of combustion usually smaller in magnitude than the data-book value?* Heat is lost to the surroundings, combustion may be incomplete, and water may not be in its standard state, so the temperature rise is lower.

## 3. Bond energies

$$
\Delta H = \sum(\text{bonds broken}) - \sum(\text{bonds formed}).
$$

**Example 2.** $\text{H}_2 + \text{Cl}_2 \to 2\text{HCl}$: $436 + 243 - 2(432) = -185\ \text{kJ}$. (Average bond energies, gases only: approximate.)

## 4. Hess's law

$\Delta H$ for a reaction depends only on the initial and final states, not on the route.

$$
\Delta H_{\text{reaction}}^\circ = \sum\Delta H_f^\circ(\text{products}) - \sum\Delta H_f^\circ(\text{reactants}) = \sum\Delta H_c^\circ(\text{reactants}) - \sum\Delta H_c^\circ(\text{products}).
$$

When combining equations: reversing changes the sign of $\Delta H$; multiplying by $n$ multiplies $\Delta H$ by $n$.

**Example 3.** $\text{C} + \text{O}_2 \to \text{CO}_2$, $\Delta H = -394$; $\text{CO} + \tfrac12\text{O}_2 \to \text{CO}_2$, $\Delta H = -283$. For $\text{C} + \tfrac12\text{O}_2 \to \text{CO}$: $\Delta H = -394 - (-283) = -111\ \text{kJ/mol}$.

**Example 4.** $\text{CH}_4 + 2\text{O}_2 \to \text{CO}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O(l)}$ with $\Delta H_f^\circ$ of $\text{CH}_4 = -75$, $\text{CO}_2 = -394$, $\text{H}_2\text{O} = -286$: $\Delta H = [-394 + 2(-286)] - [-75] = -891\ \text{kJ/mol}$.

**Example 5.** $\text{C}_2\text{H}_4 + \text{H}_2 \to \text{C}_2\text{H}_6$ from combustion data ($-1411$, $-286$, $-1560$): $(-1411 - 286) - (-1560) = -137\ \text{kJ/mol}$.

## 5. Spontaneity (brief)

A reaction tends to be spontaneous when $\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S < 0$. Reactions that increase disorder (a gas forms, more particles) are favoured at high temperature.

## Common mistakes

- Forgetting to change the sign of $\Delta H$ when reversing.
- Including elements in the sum of $\Delta H_f^\circ$.
- Dividing $Q$ by the moles of the wrong reagent.

## Practice problems

1. $\Delta H$ for $\text{N}_2 + \text{O}_2 \to 2\text{NO}$ with bond energies $945$, $498$, $631\ \text{(N=O)}$ in $\text{kJ/mol}$.
2. Heat released by burning $0.5\ \text{mol}$ of methane ($-891\ \text{kJ/mol}$).

**Answers.** (1) $945 + 498 - 2(631) = +181\ \text{kJ}$. (2) $445.5\ \text{kJ}$.
