## What you will learn

- concentration units and conversions;
- the titration method and calculations;
- indicators, back-titration and redox titrations.

## 1. Concentration

$$
c = \frac nV \ (\text{mol/L}), \qquad \text{mass concentration } \rho = \frac mV \ (\text{g/L}), \qquad c = \frac\rho M, \qquad c_1V_1 = c_2V_2 \ (\text{dilution}).
$$

(Chinese textbooks call the amount concentration 物质的量浓度.)

**Example 1.** $4\ \text{g}$ of NaOH in $500\ \text{mL}$: $n = 0.1\ \text{mol}$, $c = 0.2\ \text{mol/L}$. Diluting $20\ \text{mL}$ of $1\ \text{mol/L}$ HCl to $100\ \text{mL}$: $0.2\ \text{mol/L}$.

**Example 2.** Convert $0.50\ \text{mol/L}$ of NaCl to g/L: $0.5 \cdot 58.5 = 29.25\ \text{g/L}$.

## 2. Titration

A titration finds the unknown concentration of a solution by reacting an exact volume with a **standard solution** of known concentration delivered from a **burette**. At the **end point** (indicator colour change) the amounts are in the ratio of the equation.

**Steps**: (1) balanced equation; (2) moles of the standard solution used (the mean of concordant titres, within $0.10\ \text{mL}$); (3) mole ratio; (4) concentration of the unknown; (5) multiply back for any dilution.

**Example 3.** $25.0\ \text{mL}$ of NaOH needs $20.0\ \text{mL}$ of $0.100\ \text{mol/L}$ HCl: $n(\text{HCl}) = 0.00200$; $c(\text{NaOH}) = \dfrac{0.00200}{0.0250} = 0.0800\ \text{mol/L}$.

**Example 4 (diluted vinegar).** $25.0\ \text{mL}$ of diluted vinegar needs $20.0\ \text{mL}$ of $0.100\ \text{mol/L}$ NaOH: $c = 0.0800\ \text{mol/L}$; if $10.0\ \text{mL}$ of vinegar was diluted to $250\ \text{mL}$, the original is $0.0800 \cdot 25 = 2.00\ \text{mol/L}$.

**Example 5.** $20.0\ \text{mL}$ of $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ needs $25.0\ \text{mL}$ of $0.200\ \text{mol/L}$ NaOH: $n(\text{NaOH}) = 0.00500$; $n(\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4) = 0.00250$; $c = 0.125\ \text{mol/L}$.

## 3. Indicators and technique

Choose the indicator to match the equivalence-point pH: strong acid–strong base: any (near pH 7); weak acid–strong base: **phenolphthalein**; strong acid–weak base: **methyl orange**. $\text{KMnO}_4$ is **self-indicating** (the pink persists at the end point).

Technique: rinse the burette with the solution it will hold, the pipette with the solution it will deliver, the conical flask with **distilled water only**; remove air bubbles from the burette tip; read at eye level to the bottom of the meniscus; swirl; add dropwise near the end point.

## 4. Redox titration and back-titration

$\text{MnO}_4^- + 5\text{Fe}^{2+} + 8\text{H}^+ \to \text{Mn}^{2+} + 5\text{Fe}^{3+} + 4\text{H}_2\text{O}$ (ratio $1 : 5$). **Back-titration**: add an excess of a reagent to an insoluble sample (such as an antacid tablet), then titrate the excess to find how much reacted.

**Example 6.** A $25.0\ \text{mL}$ sample of $\text{Fe}^{2+}$ needs $20.0\ \text{mL}$ of $0.0200\ \text{mol/L}$ $\text{KMnO}_4$: $n(\text{MnO}_4^-) = 4.0 \times 10^{-4}$; $n(\text{Fe}^{2+}) = 2.0 \times 10^{-3}$; $c = 0.080\ \text{mol/L}$.

## Common mistakes

- Using the wrong mole ratio ($\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ with NaOH is $1 : 2$).
- Rinsing the conical flask with the solution.
- Using an outlying titre in the mean.

## Practice problems

1. $20.0\ \text{mL}$ of HCl neutralises $25.0\ \text{mL}$ of $0.200\ \text{mol/L}$ NaOH: $c(\text{HCl})$.
2. Mass of NaOH for $250\ \text{mL}$ of $0.10\ \text{mol/L}$.

**Answers.** (1) $0.250\ \text{mol/L}$. (2) $1.0\ \text{g}$.
