## What you will learn

- the model of metallic bonding;
- properties of metals and alloys;
- trends in melting points.

## 1. The model

A metal consists of a **lattice of positive metal ions** surrounded by a "sea" of **delocalised electrons** (the outer electrons of the metal atoms). **Metallic bonding** is the strong electrostatic attraction between the positive ions and the delocalised electrons.

## 2. Properties explained

- **Electrical conductivity**: the delocalised electrons move when a voltage is applied (also good thermal conduction).
- **Malleable and ductile**: layers of ions can slide over each other while the electron sea holds them together.
- **High melting and boiling points** (strong bonds), generally; shiny; high density.
- Strength of bonding increases with the **number of delocalised electrons per atom** and with smaller, more highly charged ions: $\text{Na} < \text{Mg} < \text{Al}$ in melting point ($98$, $650$, $660\ ^\circ\text{C}$).

**Example 1.** *Why is copper a good electrical conductor?* Its delocalised electrons are free to move through the lattice and carry charge.

**Example 2.** *Why does magnesium have a higher melting point than sodium?* Mg gives two delocalised electrons per atom and forms $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ ions, so the attraction is stronger.

## 3. Alloys

An **alloy** is a mixture of a metal with other elements. The atoms of different sizes **disrupt the regular layers**, making it harder for them to slide: alloys are **harder and stronger** than pure metals. Examples: **steel** (iron with carbon; stainless steel with Cr, Ni), **bronze** (Cu + Sn), **brass** (Cu + Zn), solder, duralumin.

**Example 3.** *Why is steel harder than pure iron?* Carbon atoms of different size distort the iron lattice and stop layers from sliding.

## 4. Uses

Copper: wires, pipes. Aluminium: aircraft, cans (low density; protective oxide). Iron/steel: construction. Gold: jewellery, electronics (unreactive).

## Common mistakes

- Saying metals contain free *ions* that conduct.
- Confusing metallic with ionic bonding.
- Forgetting the number of delocalised electrons in trends.

## Practice problems

1. What holds the particles together in a metal?
2. Why can metals be bent without breaking?

**Answers.** (1) Attraction between positive ions and the sea of delocalised electrons. (2) Layers of ions can slide while the electrons keep them bonded.
