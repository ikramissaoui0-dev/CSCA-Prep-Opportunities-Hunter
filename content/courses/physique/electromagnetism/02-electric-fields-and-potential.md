## What you will learn

- electric field, field lines and the field of a point charge;
- electric potential energy, potential and potential difference;
- uniform fields, equipotentials and the motion of charges.

## 1. Electric field

$\vec E = \dfrac{\vec F}{q}$ (force per unit positive test charge), unit $\text{N/C} = \text{V/m}$.

- Point charge $Q$: $E = \dfrac{kQ}{r^2}$, radial (away from $+$, toward $-$).
- Superposition: add the fields as **vectors**.
- Between parallel plates with voltage $U$ and separation $d$: $E = \dfrac Ud$ (uniform).
- Field lines start on positive charges, end on negative ones, never cross, and are denser where the field is stronger. In a conductor in electrostatic equilibrium $E = 0$ inside and charge resides on the surface.

**Example 1.** A test charge $q = +2 \times 10^{-8}\ \text{C}$ feels $4 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{N}$ due east: $E = 2 \times 10^4\ \text{N/C}$ east.

**Example 2.** At the midpoint between $+Q$ and $-Q$ (separation $2a$): $E = \dfrac{2kQ}{a^2}$ toward $-Q$. Between $+Q$ and $+Q$: $E = 0$.

## 2. Potential energy and potential

- Potential energy of two point charges: $E_p = \dfrac{kq_1q_2}r$ (negative for opposite charges).
- **Potential** $\varphi = \dfrac{E_p}q$ (volts); for a point charge $\varphi = \dfrac{kQ}r$, a **scalar**: add with signs.
- Potential difference $U_{AB} = \varphi_A - \varphi_B$; work by the field moving $q$ from $A$ to $B$: $W_{AB} = qU_{AB} = E_{pA} - E_{pB}$ (the field's work equals the *decrease* of potential energy).
- In a uniform field: $U = Ed$ along the field direction. The field points toward **decreasing** potential; equipotential surfaces are perpendicular to the field lines.
- $1\ \text{eV} = 1.60 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}$.

**Example 3.** Moving $q = -2 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{C}$ from $A$ to $B$ with the field doing $-4 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{J}$: $U_{AB} = \dfrac{-4 \times 10^{-4}}{-2 \times 10^{-6}} = 200\ \text{V}$, so $A$ is at $200\ \text{V}$ above $B$.

**Example 4 (earthing).** Two plates $4\ \text{cm}$ apart at $200\ \text{V}$ with the negative plate earthed: $E = 5000\ \text{V/m}$; a point $1\ \text{cm}$ from the negative plate has $\varphi = 50\ \text{V}$.

## 3. Charges in fields

- **Accelerated from rest through $U$**: $\tfrac12mv^2 = qU$, so $v = \sqrt{\dfrac{2qU}m}$. An electron through $100\ \text{V}$: $5.9 \times 10^6\ \text{m/s}$.
- **Entering perpendicular to a uniform field** (between plates): constant velocity along the plates, uniform acceleration $a = \dfrac{qE}m$ across them: a parabola (like a projectile).
- **Equilibrium of a droplet**: $qE = mg$. With the upper plate positive, $E$ points down, so the droplet must be **negative**: $q = \dfrac{mg}E$.

## 4. Conductors and applications

Excess charge sits on the outer surface; the field is strongest at sharp points (lightning rods, corona discharge); a hollow conductor shields its interior (a Faraday cage).

## Common mistakes

- Adding potentials as vectors or fields as scalars.
- Using $E = U/d$ for non-uniform fields.
- Forgetting the sign of the charge when deciding if the energy rises or falls.

## Practice problems

1. Field $0.5\ \text{m}$ from a $5\ \text{nC}$ charge.
2. Plate voltage $200\ \text{V}$, spacing $2\ \text{cm}$: $E$ and the force on an electron.
3. Speed of a proton accelerated through $1000\ \text{V}$ ($m = 1.67 \times 10^{-27}\ \text{kg}$).

**Answers.** (1) $180\ \text{N/C}$. (2) $10^4\ \text{V/m}$; $1.6 \times 10^{-15}\ \text{N}$. (3) $\sqrt{\dfrac{2 \cdot 1.6 \times 10^{-19} \cdot 1000}{1.67 \times 10^{-27}}} \approx 4.4 \times 10^5\ \text{m/s}$.
