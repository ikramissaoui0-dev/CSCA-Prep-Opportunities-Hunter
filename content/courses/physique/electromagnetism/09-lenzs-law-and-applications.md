## What you will learn

- the statement of Lenz's law and the four-step method;
- typical situations: magnets and coils, moving loops, eddy currents;
- the right-hand rule for a moving rod.

## 1. Lenz's law

The induced current flows in the direction that **opposes the change** in flux that produced it (the effect opposes the cause). This is conservation of energy: if the induced current aided the change, energy would be created from nothing.

**Method.**

1. Find the direction of the original field through the loop.
2. Is the flux **increasing** or **decreasing**?
3. The induced field **opposes the change**: opposite to the original if the flux increases, the same direction if it decreases.
4. Use the right-hand grip rule to get the current direction.

**Example 1 (magnet and coil).** The N pole of a magnet moves toward a coil: the flux directed into the coil increases, so the induced field points out toward the magnet, making the near face of the coil an N pole — the coil **repels** the approaching magnet. When the magnet moves away, the near face becomes an S pole and attracts it: the induced effects always *oppose the motion*.

**Example 2 (loop pulled out of a field).** A loop is pulled out of a field pointing into the page: the flux decreases, so the induced field is also into the page: a **clockwise** current.

**Example 3 (loop entering a field).** A loop entering a field pointing out of the page: flux (out) increases, the induced field points into the page: clockwise.

## 2. Applications

- **Magnet falling through a copper tube**: induced eddy currents oppose the fall, so it falls slowly.
- **Eddy currents**: induced circulating currents in bulk conductors; used in induction cooktops and magnetic braking, but they waste energy in transformer cores (hence laminated cores).
- **Electromagnetic damping** in galvanometers and balances.
- **Generators and microphones** use the same principle.

## 3. Moving rod: the right-hand rule

For a rod moving through a field, use the **right-hand rule** (右手定则): palm facing the N pole of $\vec B$, thumb along the velocity, the fingers show the induced current direction (the rod acts as a source, current flows from the negative to the positive terminal inside it). The magnetic force on the induced current opposes the motion, which is Lenz's law again.

**Example 4.** A rod sliding to the right on rails in a field pointing into the page: the right-hand rule gives the current upward in the rod; the force on that current points left, opposing the motion.

## 4. Combined problems

Induction problems link Faraday's law (magnitude) and Lenz's law (direction): find $\varepsilon$, the current $I = \dfrac\varepsilon R$, then forces, power and heat; check that the mechanical power supplied equals the electrical power dissipated.

## Common mistakes

- Saying the induced field is always opposite to the external field (it opposes the *change*).
- Confusing the right-hand rule (induction) with the left-hand rule (force).
- Forgetting that a loop in a *steady* field has no induced current.

## Practice problems

1. A magnet's S pole is pulled away from a coil. Which pole does the near end of the coil become?
2. A loop in a field into the page has a field that increases: current direction.

**Answers.** (1) The coil's near end becomes an N pole (it attracts the S pole to oppose the removal). (2) Induced field out of the page, so the current is counter-clockwise.
