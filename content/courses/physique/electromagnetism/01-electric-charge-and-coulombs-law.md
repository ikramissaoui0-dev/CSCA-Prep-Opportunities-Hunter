## What you will learn

- charge, conservation and quantisation;
- Coulomb's law and the superposition of forces;
- three-charge problems and charged spheres in contact.

## 1. Charge

There are two kinds of charge: positive and negative. Like charges repel, unlike charges attract. Charge is **conserved** (never created or destroyed) and **quantised**: every charge is a whole multiple of $e = 1.60 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}$. Charging means moving electrons (friction, contact, induction); the particle that moves is the electron. Conductors let charge move; insulators do not.

**Example 1.** A charge of $-3.2 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{C}$ means $\dfrac{3.2 \times 10^{-6}}{1.6 \times 10^{-19}} = 2 \times 10^{13}$ excess electrons.

## 2. Coulomb's law

$$
F = k\frac{|q_1q_2|}{r^2}, \qquad k = \frac1{4\pi\varepsilon_0} = 8.99 \times 10^9\ \text{N m}^2/\text{C}^2.
$$

The force acts along the line joining the point charges; repulsive for like charges, attractive for unlike. Doubling the distance divides the force by $4$.

**Example 2.** $+3\ \mu\text{C}$ and $-5\ \mu\text{C}$, $0.3\ \text{m}$ apart: $F = 9 \times 10^9\dfrac{15 \times 10^{-12}}{0.09} = 1.5\ \text{N}$, attractive.

**Example 3.** Two $2\ \mu\text{C}$ charges at $0.3\ \text{m}$: $F = 0.4\ \text{N}$ (repulsive).

## 3. Superposition

With several charges, the force on one is the **vector sum** of the forces from the others. Along a line, give each force a sign (direction) and add.

**Example 4.** $+2\ \mu\text{C}$ at $x = 0$, $-4\ \mu\text{C}$ at $x = 0.2\ \text{m}$ and $+1\ \mu\text{C}$ at $x = 0.1\ \text{m}$ (middle). The $+2$ pushes the middle charge in $+x$: $F_1 = 9 \times 10^9\dfrac{2 \times 10^{-12}}{0.01} = 1.8\ \text{N}$; the $-4$ attracts it in $+x$: $F_2 = 9 \times 10^9\dfrac{4 \times 10^{-12}}{0.01} = 3.6\ \text{N}$. Net $5.4\ \text{N}$ in $+x$.

**Equilibrium of a third charge.** Between two like charges the forces can cancel: place $q_3$ at distance $x$ from $q_1$ with $\dfrac{q_1}{x^2} = \dfrac{q_2}{(d - x)^2}$ (nearer the smaller charge). Outside two unlike charges the equilibrium point lies on the side of the **smaller** charge.

## 4. Contact and induction

Two identical conducting spheres that touch share their total charge equally: $+6\ \mu\text{C}$ and $-2\ \mu\text{C}$ become $+2\ \mu\text{C}$ each. A charged rod can **induce** opposite charge on the near side of a neutral conductor (and the same sign on the far side), which is why a charged rod attracts neutral bits of paper.

**Comparison with gravity**: the electric force between electron and proton is about $10^{39}$ times the gravitational force.

## Common mistakes

- Adding forces as scalars.
- Using $\mu\text{C}$ without converting to coulombs.
- Forgetting the direction (sign) of each force.

## Practice problems

1. Force between $1\ \mu\text{C}$ charges $1\ \text{m}$ apart.
2. Two charges $+q$ and $+4q$ are $0.3\ \text{m}$ apart: where is the field zero between them?
3. Final charges when spheres $+8\ \mu\text{C}$ and $-2\ \mu\text{C}$ touch.

**Answers.** (1) $9 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{N}$. (2) $\dfrac{q}{x^2} = \dfrac{4q}{(0.3 - x)^2} \Rightarrow 0.3 - x = 2x \Rightarrow x = 0.1\ \text{m}$ from $q$. (3) $+3\ \mu\text{C}$ each.
