## What you will learn

- momentum, impulse and the impulse–momentum theorem;
- conservation of momentum and recoil;
- elastic, inelastic and perfectly inelastic collisions.

## 1. Momentum and impulse

$$
\vec p = m\vec v, \qquad \vec J = \vec F_{\text{net}}\Delta t = \Delta\vec p.
$$

Impulse is the area under the $F$–$t$ graph. A longer collision time reduces the average force for the same $\Delta p$ (airbags, crumple zones, catching a ball). Momentum is a **vector**: choose a positive direction.

**Example 1.** A $0.1\ \text{kg}$ ball hits a wall at $10\ \text{m/s}$ and rebounds at $8\ \text{m/s}$; contact $0.01\ \text{s}$: $\Delta p = 0.1(8 + 10) = 1.8\ \text{kg m/s}$, $F = 180\ \text{N}$.

## 2. Conservation of momentum

If the net external force on a system is zero (or negligible during a collision):

$$
m_1\vec v_1 + m_2\vec v_2 = m_1\vec v_1' + m_2\vec v_2'.
$$

**Example 2 (perfectly inelastic).** A $2\ \text{kg}$ cart at $6\ \text{m/s}$ hits a stationary $3\ \text{kg}$ cart and they stick: $v' = \dfrac{12}5 = 2.4\ \text{m/s}$. The kinetic energy falls from $36\ \text{J}$ to $14.4\ \text{J}$.

**Example 3 (recoil).** A $60\ \text{kg}$ person on ice throws a $5\ \text{kg}$ ball at $10\ \text{m/s}$: recoil speed $\dfrac{50}{60} = 0.83\ \text{m/s}$ in the opposite direction.

**Example 4 (ballistic pendulum).** A bullet embeds in a block: momentum is conserved in the collision, then mechanical energy is conserved as it swings up: $\tfrac12(m + M)v'^2 = (m + M)gh$.

## 3. Types of collision

| Type | Momentum | Kinetic energy |
|---|---|---|
| Elastic | conserved | conserved |
| Inelastic | conserved | partly lost |
| Perfectly inelastic | conserved | maximum loss (stick together) |

**Perfectly inelastic**: $v' = \dfrac{m_1v_1 + m_2v_2}{m_1 + m_2}$.

**Elastic, 1D**:

$$
v_1' = \frac{m_1 - m_2}{m_1 + m_2}v_1 + \frac{2m_2}{m_1 + m_2}v_2, \qquad v_2' = \frac{2m_1}{m_1 + m_2}v_1 + \frac{m_2 - m_1}{m_1 + m_2}v_2.
$$

Equal masses exchange velocities. In an elastic 1D collision the relative speed of approach equals that of separation.

**Example 5.** A $1\ \text{kg}$ ball at $4\ \text{m/s}$ strikes a stationary $3\ \text{kg}$ ball elastically: $v_1' = -2\ \text{m/s}$, $v_2' = 2\ \text{m/s}$.

**Example 6.** Equal masses: a moving ball of $1.0\ \text{kg}$ hits a stationary identical ball elastically: it stops and the other moves off at the same speed.

## 4. Explosions and 2D

In an explosion the internal forces are large and the total momentum is conserved: a body initially at rest splits into pieces with $\sum\vec p = 0$. In 2D, conserve each component separately.

## Common mistakes

- Forgetting the sign of velocity in momentum conservation.
- Applying energy conservation to an inelastic collision.
- Treating momentum as a scalar.

## Practice problems

1. A $2\ \text{kg}$ body changes velocity from $3\ \text{m/s}$ to $-1\ \text{m/s}$ in $0.5\ \text{s}$. Average force?
2. A $1000\ \text{kg}$ car at $10\ \text{m/s}$ hits a stationary $1000\ \text{kg}$ car and they lock: final speed?
3. A $50\ \text{kg}$ skater pushes a $25\ \text{kg}$ skater apart on ice; the lighter one moves at $2\ \text{m/s}$. Speed of the heavier?

**Answers.** (1) $F = \dfrac{2(-1 - 3)}{0.5} = -16\ \text{N}$. (2) $5\ \text{m/s}$. (3) $1\ \text{m/s}$ (opposite direction).
