## What you will learn

- work by a constant and a variable force;
- power, including a vehicle at constant power;
- kinetic energy and the work–energy theorem.

## 1. Work

$$
W = Fd\cos\theta \quad (\theta \text{ the angle between the force and the displacement}).
$$

Work is positive if the force helps the motion, negative if it opposes (friction), and **zero** if the force is perpendicular to the motion (normal force, centripetal force). The work of a variable force is the **area under the $F$–$x$ graph**. The total work is the sum of the work of each force.

- Gravity: $W = mgh$ (positive going down; independent of path).
- Friction: $-fs$ (proportional to path length).
- Spring: $W = -\tfrac12k(x_2^2 - x_1^2)$.

## 2. Power

$$
P = \frac Wt = Fv\cos\theta, \qquad 1\ \text{W} = 1\ \text{J/s}, \qquad \text{efficiency } \eta = \frac{P_{\text{out}}}{P_{\text{in}}}.
$$

**Vehicle at constant power** (机车启动). With rated power $P$ and constant resistance $f$: $F = \dfrac Pv$; as $v$ rises $F$ falls and $a = \dfrac{P/v - f}m$ falls to zero, where the speed is maximal:

$$
v_{\max} = \frac Pf.
$$

Example: $P = 60\ \text{kW}$, $f = 2000\ \text{N}$, $m = 1000\ \text{kg}$: $v_{\max} = 30\ \text{m/s}$; at $10\ \text{m/s}$: $F = 6000\ \text{N}$, $a = 4\ \text{m/s}^2$.

**Constant-acceleration start**: $F = f + ma$ is constant, $P = Fv$ rises until it reaches the rated power at $v_1 = \dfrac PF$; afterwards constant-power motion.

## 3. Kinetic energy and the work–energy theorem

$$
E_k = \tfrac12mv^2, \qquad W_{\text{net}} = \Delta E_k = \tfrac12mv_2^2 - \tfrac12mv_1^2.
$$

**Example 1.** A $1000\ \text{kg}$ car from $10$ to $20\ \text{m/s}$: $W = \tfrac12 \cdot 1000(400 - 100) = 150\ \text{kJ}$.

**Example 2.** A $4\ \text{kg}$ box pulled $5\ \text{m}$ by $F = 25\ \text{N}$ at $37^\circ$ above the horizontal, $\mu = 0.2$ ($g = 10$): $N = 40 - 25 \cdot 0.6 = 25\ \text{N}$, $f = 5\ \text{N}$; $W_F = 25 \cdot 5 \cdot 0.8 = 100\ \text{J}$, $W_f = -25\ \text{J}$, net $75\ \text{J}$, $v = \sqrt{2 \cdot 75/4} \approx 6.1\ \text{m/s}$.

**Example 3.** A $2\ \text{kg}$ stone thrown at $10\ \text{m/s}$ from a $15\ \text{m}$ cliff reaches the ground at $v$: $\tfrac12mv^2 = \tfrac12m \cdot 100 + mg \cdot 15 \Rightarrow v = \sqrt{100 + 300} = 20\ \text{m/s}$.

**Example 4 (stopping).** Block on a rough floor from $v_0$: $\mu mgs = \tfrac12mv_0^2 \Rightarrow s = \dfrac{v_0^2}{2\mu g}$.

## 4. Potential energy

Gravitational $E_p = mgh$ (reference level chosen), elastic $E_p = \tfrac12kx^2$. The work of a conservative force equals the *decrease* of its potential energy: $W = E_{p1} - E_{p2}$.

## Common mistakes

- Using $W = Fd$ without $\cos\theta$.
- Counting the normal force's work on an incline.
- Using the total path for gravity (only height matters).

## Practice problems

1. $W$ to lift $10\ \text{kg}$ by $3\ \text{m}$ at constant speed, and the power if it takes $5\ \text{s}$ ($g = 10$).
2. A $2\ \text{kg}$ body goes from $3$ to $5\ \text{m/s}$: net work.
3. Maximum speed of a car with $P = 40\ \text{kW}$ against a $2000\ \text{N}$ resistance.

**Answers.** (1) $300\ \text{J}$; $60\ \text{W}$. (2) $\tfrac12 \cdot 2(25 - 9) = 16\ \text{J}$. (3) $20\ \text{m/s}$.
