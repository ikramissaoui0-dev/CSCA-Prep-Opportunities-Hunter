## What you will learn

- the normal force and how it changes;
- static and kinetic friction and the coefficient $\mu$;
- friction on inclines and the direction of friction.

## 1. Normal force

The **normal force** $N$ is the contact force perpendicular to the surface; it is whatever is needed to prevent penetration. It is $mg$ only on a level surface with no vertical acceleration.

- Pushing a block with force $F$ at angle $\theta$ **below** the horizontal: $N = mg + F\sin\theta$.
- Pulling at $\theta$ **above** the horizontal: $N = mg - F\sin\theta$.
- On an incline of angle $\theta$: $N = mg\cos\theta$.

## 2. Friction

- **Kinetic (sliding) friction**: $f_k = \mu_kN$, opposing sliding.
- **Static friction**: adjusts to match the applied force up to a maximum $f_{s,\max} = \mu_sN$ ($\mu_s \ge \mu_k$). A body at rest on a rough incline experiences $f = mg\sin\theta$ (not $\mu mg\cos\theta$) unless it is about to slip.
- Friction does not depend on the contact area (for the model used here).
- Friction opposes the *relative sliding or its tendency*, not necessarily the motion: a box on an accelerating truck is pushed forward by static friction.

**Example 1.** A $10\ \text{kg}$ crate on a floor with $\mu_s = 0.5$, $\mu_k = 0.4$ ($g = 10$): maximum static friction $50\ \text{N}$. A $30\ \text{N}$ push does not move it (friction $30\ \text{N}$); a $60\ \text{N}$ push does: $a = \dfrac{60 - 40}{10} = 2\ \text{m/s}^2$.

**Example 2.** A $5\ \text{kg}$ block on a level floor ($\mu = 0.3$) pushed by $30\ \text{N}$: $f = 15\ \text{N}$, $a = 3\ \text{m/s}^2$.

## 3. Inclines

On an incline of angle $\theta$:

- sliding down with friction: $a = g(\sin\theta - \mu_k\cos\theta)$;
- the block just starts to slide when $\tan\theta = \mu_s$ (so $\mu_s = \tan\theta$ at the critical angle); at constant speed $\mu_k = \tan\theta$;
- projected **up** the slope: $a = g(\sin\theta + \mu\cos\theta)$ (both act down the slope).

**Example 3.** A block is projected up a $37^\circ$ slope with $\mu = 0.5$ ($g = 10$): deceleration $10(0.6 + 0.4) = 10\ \text{m/s}^2$. Starting at $10\ \text{m/s}$, it travels $5\ \text{m}$ up; it then slides back only if $\tan37^\circ = 0.75 > \mu_s$.

**Example 4.** Friction on a $2\ \text{kg}$ block on a $30^\circ$ slope with $\mu_k = 0.2$: $a = 10(0.5 - 0.2 \cdot 0.866) \approx 3.27\ \text{m/s}^2$.

## 4. Practical points

Friction can be useful (walking, brakes) or wasteful (machines). Lubrication, rollers and streamlining reduce it. Air resistance increases with speed; at **terminal velocity** the drag equals the weight and $a = 0$.

## Common mistakes

- Using $\mu N$ for static friction as if it were always at its maximum.
- Taking $N = mg$ on an incline or with an angled force.
- Pointing friction along the velocity instead of against the relative sliding.

## Practice problems

1. At what angle does a block with $\mu_s = 0.75$ begin to slide?
2. A $2\ \text{kg}$ block, $\mu_k = 0.25$, is pulled by $10\ \text{N}$ horizontally ($g = 10$). $a$?
3. Static friction on a $5\ \text{kg}$ block at rest on a $30^\circ$ incline.

**Answers.** (1) $\theta = \arctan0.75 \approx 37^\circ$. (2) $f = 5$, $a = 2.5\ \text{m/s}^2$. (3) $mg\sin30^\circ = 25\ \text{N}$ (with $g = 10$).
