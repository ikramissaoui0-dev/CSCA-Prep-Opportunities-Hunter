## What you will learn

- the three statements of molecular kinetic theory;
- evidence: diffusion and Brownian motion;
- molecular size, Avogadro's number and the force between molecules.

## 1. The three statements

1. All matter is made of **molecules** (or atoms), about $10^{-10}\ \text{m}$ in size.
2. Molecules are in constant, random **thermal motion**; the motion is more vigorous at higher temperature.
3. Molecules **attract and repel** each other.

## 2. Evidence

- **Diffusion**: gases and liquids mix by themselves (a drop of ink spreading, a smell crossing a room). It is faster at higher temperature and in gases than in liquids; solids diffuse very slowly.
- **Brownian motion**: tiny visible particles (pollen, smoke) suspended in a fluid zig-zag randomly because they are bombarded unevenly by molecules. It is more vigorous for **smaller particles** and at **higher temperatures**; it is *not* the motion of the molecules themselves but evidence for it.

## 3. Molecular size and numbers

$N_A = 6.02 \times 10^{23}\ \text{mol}^{-1}$. For water: $M = 18\ \text{g/mol}$; $1\ \text{cm}^3 = 1\ \text{g}$ contains $\dfrac1{18} \cdot 6.02 \times 10^{23} = 3.3 \times 10^{22}$ molecules.

**Example 1.** Volume per molecule: $\dfrac{10^{-6}\ \text{m}^3}{3.3 \times 10^{22}} = 3 \times 10^{-29}\ \text{m}^3$. Treating each molecule as a cube: edge $\approx 3 \times 10^{-10}\ \text{m}$, so the diameter is a few times $10^{-10}\ \text{m}$.

**Example 2.** Oil-film estimate: one drop of $V = 10^{-9}\ \text{m}^3$ spreads over $0.5\ \text{m}^2$ as a single layer: thickness $\dfrac V A = 2 \times 10^{-9}\ \text{m}$ gives an upper bound for the molecule's size.

## 4. Forces between molecules

The force depends on the separation $r$, with the equilibrium separation $r_0 \approx 10^{-10}\ \text{m}$:

- $r = r_0$: attraction and repulsion balance, the net force is zero, the potential energy is **minimum**;
- $r < r_0$: repulsion dominates and rises steeply (liquids and solids resist compression);
- $r > r_0$: attraction dominates, falling to nearly zero beyond about $10r_0$.

**Example 3.** A molecule $B$ starts far from a fixed molecule $A$ and moves toward it. While $r > r_0$ the attraction speeds $B$ up (potential energy falls, kinetic energy rises); at $r = r_0$ the net force is zero and the speed is maximum; for $r < r_0$ repulsion slows $B$ down until it stops momentarily at the closest approach and is pushed back.

**States of matter.** In gases the molecules are far apart, so the forces are negligible; in liquids and solids the forces keep the molecules close.

## Common mistakes

- Treating Brownian motion as the motion of molecules rather than evidence for it.
- Thinking the molecular forces are only attractive.
- Using $N_A$ with mass in kg.

## Practice problems

1. Number of molecules in $9\ \text{g}$ of water.
2. At what separation is the potential energy of two molecules a minimum?

**Answers.** (1) $0.5\ \text{mol}$: $3 \times 10^{23}$. (2) $r = r_0 \approx 10^{-10}\ \text{m}$.
