## What you will learn

- the same process on $p$–$V$, $p$–$T$ and $V$–$T$ graphs;
- reading temperature changes from graphs;
- the "farther from the axes means hotter" rule.

## 1. The equation of state and its graphs

$pV = nRT$ and $\dfrac{p_1V_1}{T_1} = \dfrac{p_2V_2}{T_2}$ for fixed $n$.

| Process | $p$–$V$ | $p$–$T$ (kelvin) | $V$–$T$ (kelvin) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Isothermal ($T$ fixed) | hyperbola | vertical line | vertical line |
| Isobaric ($p$ fixed) | horizontal line | horizontal line | straight line through the origin |
| Isochoric ($V$ fixed) | vertical line | straight line through the origin | horizontal line |

On a $p$–$T$ or $V$–$T$ graph, "through the origin" must be in **kelvin**; with Celsius the line meets the axis at $-273\ ^\circ\text{C}$.

## 2. Temperature from a $p$–$V$ graph

Since $T \propto pV$, a state farther from the axes has a higher temperature. A hyperbola $pV = $ const is an isotherm; isotherms farther from the origin are hotter. An isobar ($p$ const) with a larger $p$ is higher; an isochor with a larger $V$ is farther right.

**Example 1.** A gas goes from $A(2 \times 10^5\ \text{Pa}, 1\ \text{L})$ to $B(10^5\ \text{Pa}, 4\ \text{L})$: In units of $10^5\ \text{Pa}\cdot\text{L}$, $p_AV_A = 2$ and $p_BV_B = 4$, so $T_B = 2T_A$. Along the straight line joining $A$ and $B$ the temperature need not change monotonically: with $p = \dfrac{7 - V}{3}$ the product $pV$ is largest at $V = 3.5\ \text{L}$ (value $4.08$), slightly above its value at $B$, so the temperature rises past $T_B$ and then falls back to $T_B$.

**Example 2.** A bubble rises from $20\ \text{m}$ depth ($3\ \text{atm}$) to the surface at constant temperature: $V_2 = 3V_1$.

**Example 3.** A tyre at $2\ \text{atm}$ gauge ($3\ \text{atm}$ absolute) at $27\ ^\circ\text{C}$ heats to $57\ ^\circ\text{C}$: $p = 3.3\ \text{atm}$ absolute.

## 3. Work and heat from the graph

The work is the area under the $p$–$V$ curve. For a process with $\Delta T$ known: $\Delta U = nC_V\Delta T$ and then $Q = \Delta U + W$.

**Example 4.** Isobaric expansion at $10^5\ \text{Pa}$ from $1$ to $3\ \text{L}$: $W = 200\ \text{J}$; $T$ triples (volume triples); for $0.1\ \text{mol}$ of monatomic gas, $\Delta U = \tfrac32 \cdot 0.1 \cdot 8.31 \cdot \Delta T$ with $T_1 = \dfrac{pV}{nR} = 120\ \text{K}$, $\Delta T = 240\ \text{K}$, so $\Delta U = 299\ \text{J}$ and $Q = 499\ \text{J}$.

## 4. A naming trap

Charles's and Gay-Lussac's laws are named differently in Chinese and Western textbooks. Always identify the process by the fixed variable and the graph shape, not by the name.

## Common mistakes

- Reading a $p$–$T$ line through the origin when $T$ is in Celsius.
- Assuming the temperature always changes monotonically along a straight $p$–$V$ line.
- Forgetting that the area under the curve is work (not heat).

## Practice problems

1. A gas at $2\ \text{atm}$, $4\ \text{L}$, $300\ \text{K}$ is compressed isothermally to $1\ \text{L}$: pressure.
2. Which state is hotter: $(4\ \text{atm}, 1\ \text{L})$ or $(1\ \text{atm}, 3\ \text{L})$?

**Answers.** (1) $8\ \text{atm}$. (2) $pV = 4$ vs $3$: the first.
