## What you will learn

- travelling waves and reflection at boundaries;
- standing waves: nodes, antinodes and harmonics on strings and in pipes;
- beats, resonance and the Doppler effect.

## 1. Travelling waves

$v = f\lambda$, $T = \dfrac1f$. A wave transports energy, not matter. The speed on a string is $v = \sqrt{\dfrac T\mu}$ ($T$ tension, $\mu$ mass per length); the speed of sound in air is about $340\ \text{m/s}$ (faster in liquids and solids), independent of frequency. At a **fixed end** a wave reflects with a phase change of $\pi$ (a node); at a **free end** with no phase change (an antinode).

## 2. Standing waves

Two identical waves travelling in opposite directions form a **standing wave**: **nodes** (zero amplitude) and **antinodes** (maximum amplitude) alternate, with spacing $\dfrac\lambda2$ between neighbouring nodes.

| System | Ends | Allowed wavelengths | Frequencies |
|---|---|---|---|
| String of length $L$ fixed at both ends | node–node | $L = n\dfrac\lambda2$ | $f_n = \dfrac{nv}{2L}$ |
| Pipe open at both ends | antinode–antinode | $L = n\dfrac\lambda2$ | $f_n = \dfrac{nv}{2L}$ |
| Pipe closed at one end | node–antinode | $L = (2n - 1)\dfrac\lambda4$ | $f = \dfrac{(2n - 1)v}{4L}$ (odd harmonics only) |

$n = 1$ is the **fundamental**; higher ones are the harmonics (overtones).

**Example 1.** A string of $0.65\ \text{m}$ with $v = 260\ \text{m/s}$: $f_1 = \dfrac{260}{1.3} = 200\ \text{Hz}$; then $400$, $600\ \text{Hz}$. Pressing a fret shortens $L$ and raises $f$.

**Example 2.** A closed pipe of $0.17\ \text{m}$ ($v = 340\ \text{m/s}$): $f_1 = \dfrac{340}{0.68} = 500\ \text{Hz}$, then $1500\ \text{Hz}$, $2500\ \text{Hz}$.

**Example 3.** An open pipe of $0.5\ \text{m}$: $f_1 = \dfrac{340}{1.0} = 340\ \text{Hz}$.

## 3. Resonance and beats

**Resonance**: driving a system at its natural frequency produces a large amplitude (a swing, a glass shattered by sound, a bridge). **Beats**: two nearby frequencies $f_1$, $f_2$ give a periodic variation in loudness at the **beat frequency** $|f_1 - f_2|$ (used to tune instruments).

## 4. Doppler effect

If the source and observer approach, the observed frequency is higher:

$$
f' = f\,\frac{v + v_o}{v - v_s} \ (\text{approach: observer toward source, source toward observer}).
$$

For separation, change the signs. **Example 4.** A siren at $700\ \text{Hz}$ approaching at $20\ \text{m/s}$ ($v = 340$): $f' = 700\cdot\dfrac{340}{320} \approx 744\ \text{Hz}$; after it passes, $700\cdot\dfrac{340}{360} \approx 661\ \text{Hz}$.

## 5. Sound characteristics

Pitch depends on frequency, loudness on amplitude (intensity level $\beta = 10\log_{10}\dfrac I{I_0}$ dB, $I_0 = 10^{-12}\ \text{W/m}^2$; ten times the intensity adds $10\ \text{dB}$), timbre on the harmonics. An echo: $d = \dfrac{vt}2$.

## Common mistakes

- Confusing open and closed pipe patterns.
- Believing the frequency changes at a boundary.
- Mixing the Doppler signs.

## Practice problems

1. String fixed at both ends, $f_1 = 100\ \text{Hz}$: next two harmonics.
2. Beat frequency of $440\ \text{Hz}$ and $444\ \text{Hz}$.
3. An echo is heard after $0.4\ \text{s}$: distance to the wall.

**Answers.** (1) $200$, $300\ \text{Hz}$. (2) $4\ \text{Hz}$. (3) $68\ \text{m}$.
