## What you will learn

- the conditions and equations of SHM;
- the spring–mass system and the simple pendulum;
- energy in SHM, resonance and damping; waves from vibrations.

## 1. Simple harmonic motion

SHM occurs when the restoring force is proportional to the displacement: $F = -kx$, so $a = -\dfrac kmx$. Then

$$
x = A\cos(\omega t + \varphi), \qquad v_{\max} = A\omega, \qquad a_{\max} = A\omega^2, \qquad T = \frac{2\pi}\omega, \qquad f = \frac1T.
$$

At the equilibrium position the speed is maximum and the acceleration zero; at the extremes the speed is zero and $|a|$ is maximum. The **period and frequency do not depend on the amplitude**.

## 2. Spring and pendulum

- **Mass on a spring**: $\omega = \sqrt{\dfrac km}$, $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac mk}$ (independent of $g$; a vertical spring has the same $T$).
- **Simple pendulum** (small angles, $\lesssim 5^\circ$): $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac Lg}$, independent of mass and amplitude.
- Springs in parallel: $k = k_1 + k_2$; in series: $\dfrac1k = \dfrac1{k_1} + \dfrac1{k_2}$.

**Example 1.** A pendulum's length is quadrupled: $T$ doubles. A pendulum completing $20$ oscillations in $40\ \text{s}$: $T = 2\ \text{s}$, $f = 0.5\ \text{Hz}$, $L = \dfrac{gT^2}{4\pi^2} \approx 1\ \text{m}$. On a mountain (smaller $g$) a pendulum clock runs slow.

**Example 2.** A $0.4\ \text{kg}$ mass on $k = 100\ \text{N/m}$: $T = 2\pi\sqrt{0.004} \approx 0.40\ \text{s}$; with $A = 5\ \text{cm}$, $v_{\max} = A\omega = 0.79\ \text{m/s}$.

## 3. Energy

$E = \tfrac12kA^2$ is constant; at displacement $x$: $E_p = \tfrac12kx^2$, $E_k = \tfrac12k(A^2 - x^2)$, $v = \omega\sqrt{A^2 - x^2}$. At $x = \dfrac A2$, $E_p = \tfrac14E$ and $E_k = \tfrac34E$.

**Graph reading.** On an $x$–$t$ graph the slope is the velocity: maximal at the equilibrium crossing, zero at the peaks.

## 4. Damping and resonance

Friction removes energy and the amplitude decays (**damped** vibration). A **driven** oscillator oscillates at the driving frequency; the amplitude is largest when the driving frequency equals the natural frequency (**resonance**).

## 5. From vibrations to waves

A wave is a vibration passing from point to point: each point oscillates with period $T$ while the pattern advances $\lambda$ in one period. A wave with $f = 50\ \text{Hz}$ and $\lambda = 4\ \text{m}$ has $v = 200\ \text{m/s}$. Points separated by $\dfrac\lambda2$ oscillate in antiphase. A $y$–$x$ graph is a snapshot (read $\lambda$); a $y$–$t$ graph follows one point (read $T$). *Why does an approaching siren sound higher?* The wave crests reach the observer more often (Doppler effect).

## Common mistakes

- Thinking the SHM period depends on the amplitude.
- Using $T = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}$ for large angles.
- Mixing up $x$–$t$ and $v$–$t$ phases.

## Practice problems

1. Find $L$ for a pendulum with $T = 2\ \text{s}$ ($g = 9.8$).
2. The mass on a spring is quadrupled: period change.
3. $A = 4\ \text{cm}$, $\omega = 10\ \text{rad/s}$: $v_{\max}$ and $a_{\max}$.

**Answers.** (1) $0.99\ \text{m}$. (2) $T$ doubles. (3) $0.4\ \text{m/s}$; $4\ \text{m/s}^2$.
