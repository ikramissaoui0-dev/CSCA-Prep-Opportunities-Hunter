## What you will learn

- the three areas examined and how to approach each;
- worked strategy examples and traps;
- a compact formula sheet.

## 1. The three areas

1. **The photoelectric effect** (and wave–particle duality).
2. **Atomic structure** (Rutherford, Bohr levels, spectra).
3. **Fundamentals of nuclear physics** (decay, half-life, binding energy, fission and fusion).

In our reference mock papers modern physics takes about $5$ of $48$ questions.

## 2. Strategy

- **Work in electron-volts**: $E(\text{eV}) = \dfrac{1240}{\lambda(\text{nm})}$.
- **Photoelectric**: intensity changes the *number* of electrons; frequency changes their *energy*; check $f > f_0$ first.
- **Bohr levels** are negative; a transition emits $E_{\text{high}} - E_{\text{low}}$. A photon is absorbed only if it matches a gap exactly (or exceeds the ionisation energy).
- **Nuclear equations**: balance mass number and charge separately.
- **Half-life**: count $n = t/T_{1/2}$ and use $\left(\tfrac12\right)^n$.
- **Mass defect**: $E = \Delta m \times 931.5\ \text{MeV/u}$.
- **Units**: do not mix $\text{eV}$ with $\text{J}$, $\text{u}$ with $\text{kg}$.

## 3. Worked strategy

*"Light of the same frequency but twice the intensity falls on the same metal."* $E_{k,\max}$ and the stopping voltage are **unchanged**; the saturation current doubles.

*"Hydrogen atoms in $n = 3$ return to the ground state, and the light falls on a metal with $W_0 = 2.3\ \text{eV}$."* Lines: $3 \to 2$ ($1.89\ \text{eV}$, too low), $2 \to 1$ ($10.2\ \text{eV}$) and $3 \to 1$ ($12.09\ \text{eV}$): two lines can eject electrons.

*"A sample's activity falls to $\tfrac1{16}$ of its starting value in $20$ days."* $4$ half-lives: $T_{1/2} = 5$ days.

## 4. Formula sheet

$E = hf = \dfrac{hc}\lambda$; $hf = W_0 + E_{k,\max}$; $eU_c = E_{k,\max}$; $\lambda = \dfrac hp$; $E_n = -\dfrac{13.6}{n^2}\ \text{eV}$; $hf = E_m - E_n$; $N = N_0\left(\tfrac12\right)^{t/T_{1/2}}$; $E = \Delta mc^2$ with $1\ \text{u} = 931.5\ \text{MeV}/c^2$.

## 5. Traps

- Mixing units (eV vs J).
- Forgetting the incoming particle in a nuclear equation.
- Believing brighter light gives faster photoelectrons.
- Treating half-life as applying to one nucleus.

## Quick self-test

1. Photon energy for $400\ \text{nm}$.
2. Energy emitted when hydrogen falls from $n = 3$ to $n = 1$.
3. A $64\ \text{g}$ sample with $T_{1/2} = 3$ days after $12$ days.

**Answers.** (1) $3.1\ \text{eV}$. (2) $12.09\ \text{eV}$. (3) $4\ \text{g}$.
