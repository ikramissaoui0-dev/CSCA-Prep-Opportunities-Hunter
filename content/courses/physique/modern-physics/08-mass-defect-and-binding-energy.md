## What you will learn

- mass–energy equivalence and mass defect;
- binding energy and binding energy per nucleon;
- energy released in nuclear reactions.

## 1. Mass defect

The mass of a nucleus is **less** than the sum of the masses of its separate nucleons. The difference is the **mass defect**:

$$
\Delta m = (Zm_p + Nm_n) - m_{\text{nucleus}}.
$$

## 2. Mass–energy equivalence

$$
E = mc^2, \qquad \Delta E = \Delta m\,c^2, \qquad 1\ \text{u} \leftrightarrow 931.5\ \text{MeV}.
$$

The **binding energy** $E_B = \Delta m\,c^2$ is the energy needed to separate the nucleus into free nucleons (and the energy released when it forms).

**Example 1.** Tritium ${}^3_1\text{H}$ (nuclear mass $3.0150\ \text{u}$, $m_p = 1.0073$, $m_n = 1.0087$): $\Delta m = 1.0073 + 2(1.0087) - 3.0150 = 0.0097\ \text{u}$; $E_B = 0.0097 \cdot 931.5 = 9.0\ \text{MeV}$, about $3.0\ \text{MeV}$ per nucleon.

**Example 2.** A reaction with a mass defect of $0.2\ \text{u}$ releases $186\ \text{MeV}$.

**Example 3.** A reaction in which the mass decreases by $1\ \text{g}$ releases $9 \times 10^{13}\ \text{J}$ (equal to $\Delta m c^2 = 10^{-3} \cdot (3 \times 10^8)^2$).

## 3. Binding energy per nucleon

The binding energy per nucleon $E_B/A$ measures stability. It rises steeply for light nuclei, peaks at about $8.8\ \text{MeV}$ near iron ($A \approx 56$), then falls slowly for heavy nuclei ($\approx 7.6\ \text{MeV}$ for uranium). The more tightly bound the nucleus, the more stable.

- **Fission** of a heavy nucleus into medium ones increases $E_B/A$: energy is released.
- **Fusion** of light nuclei into heavier ones increases $E_B/A$: energy is released.

## 4. Estimating energy released

**Example 4.** Splitting ${}^{235}\text{U}$ into two medium nuclei: $E_B/A$ rises from about $7.6$ to $8.5\ \text{MeV}$, a gain of about $0.9\ \text{MeV}$ per nucleon, so $235 \times 0.9 \approx 200\ \text{MeV}$ per fission. With $E/A \approx 7$ and $8$ (a gain of $1$): $235\ \text{MeV}$.

**Example 5.** One kilogram of ${}^{235}\text{U}$ contains $\dfrac{1000}{235} \cdot 6.02 \times 10^{23} \approx 2.6 \times 10^{24}$ nuclei, so full fission would release $\approx 5 \times 10^{26}\ \text{MeV} \approx 8 \times 10^{13}\ \text{J}$, about $3 \times 10^6$ times the energy in $1\ \text{kg}$ of coal.

**Example 6 (fusion).** ${}^2_1\text{H} + {}^2_1\text{H} \to {}^3_2\text{He} + {}^1_0n$: $\Delta m = 2(2.0141) - (3.0160 + 1.0087) = 0.0035\ \text{u}$, releasing $3.3\ \text{MeV}$.

## Common mistakes

- Using atomic and nuclear masses inconsistently.
- Forgetting that the *products* have less total mass than the reactants.
- Mixing $\text{u}$, $\text{MeV}/c^2$ and kg.

## Practice problems

1. Energy for a mass defect of $0.01\ \text{u}$.
2. Mass equivalent of $931.5\ \text{MeV}$.

**Answers.** (1) $9.3\ \text{MeV}$. (2) $1\ \text{u}$.
