## What you will learn

- continuous, emission and absorption spectra;
- the hydrogen series and the Balmer formula;
- spectral analysis.

## 1. Types of spectra

- **Continuous spectrum**: from hot solids, liquids or dense gases (an incandescent lamp): all colours.
- **Bright-line (emission) spectrum**: from a hot, low-pressure gas: bright lines on a dark background at wavelengths characteristic of the element.
- **Absorption spectrum**: a continuous source shining through a cooler gas: **dark lines** at exactly the wavelengths the gas would emit.

A **spectroscope** (prism or grating) separates the colours. Each element has its own line pattern, used in **spectral analysis** to identify elements in samples and stars (helium was first found in the Sun's spectrum). The dark Fraunhofer lines in sunlight come from absorption in the Sun's atmosphere.

## 2. Origin of lines

An atom emits a photon when an electron falls from a higher level to a lower one: $hf = E_m - E_n$. Only transitions between allowed levels occur, so only certain frequencies appear. In absorption the same frequencies are removed from the incident light.

## 3. Hydrogen series

| Series | Final level | Region |
|---|---|---|
| Lyman | $n = 1$ | ultraviolet |
| Balmer | $n = 2$ | visible |
| Paschen | $n = 3$ | infrared |

**Balmer formula**: $\dfrac1\lambda = R\left(\dfrac1{2^2} - \dfrac1{n^2}\right)$, $n = 3, 4, 5, \dots$, with $R = 1.097 \times 10^7\ \text{m}^{-1}$. $n = 3$ gives $656\ \text{nm}$ (red, the **longest** wavelength); $n \to \infty$ gives $364.6\ \text{nm}$ (the **series limit**, the shortest).

**Example 1.** The ratio of the shortest to the longest Balmer wavelength: $\dfrac{1/4 - 1/9}{1/4} = \dfrac59$.

**Example 2.** Using $\dfrac1\lambda = R\left(\dfrac14 - \dfrac1{16}\right)$: $\lambda = 486\ \text{nm}$.

## 4. White light through cool hydrogen

An absorption spectrum appears, with dark lines at $656$, $486$, $434\ \text{nm}$, ... Only photons matching a gap from the ground state are absorbed; in a cool gas almost all atoms are in the ground state, so only the **Lyman** lines (ultraviolet) are absorbed — the visible Balmer lines appear in absorption only in hotter gases (where $n = 2$ is populated), as in stars.

**Example 3.** A gas emits lines when excited: these are at the same wavelengths as the dark lines it produces in absorption.

## 5. Flame tests and colours

Different elements give different flame colours (Na yellow, Li red, K lilac, Cu blue-green) because of their characteristic energy levels.

## Common mistakes

- Confusing emission and absorption spectra.
- Assuming a hot dense solid gives a line spectrum.
- Forgetting that the lines of a series are labelled by the lower level.

## Practice problems

1. Which spectrum does an incandescent lamp give?
2. What produces dark lines in the solar spectrum?

**Answers.** (1) Continuous. (2) Absorption by cooler gases in the Sun's atmosphere.
