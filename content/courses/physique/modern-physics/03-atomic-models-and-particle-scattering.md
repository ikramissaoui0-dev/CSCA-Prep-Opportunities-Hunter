## What you will learn

- the plum-pudding and nuclear models;
- Rutherford's $\alpha$-scattering experiment and its conclusions;
- the closest approach and the sizes of atom and nucleus.

## 1. Early models

- **Dalton**: atoms as indivisible spheres. **Thomson** (after discovering the electron): the **plum-pudding model** — electrons embedded in a positively charged "pudding".
- **Rutherford** (1911): the **nuclear model** — a tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus with the electrons around it.

## 2. Rutherford's experiment

$\alpha$ particles were fired at a thin gold foil. Observations and conclusions:

| Observation | Conclusion |
|---|---|
| The great majority pass straight through | the atom is mostly empty space |
| A few are deflected through large angles | a small, dense, positively charged centre |
| Very few rebound almost backward | the nucleus is tiny and contains almost all the mass |

A plum-pudding atom could not produce large deflections (its charge is spread out). The nuclear model explains them by Coulomb repulsion: close to the nucleus the force is strong.

**Sizes**: atom $\approx 10^{-10}\ \text{m}$; nucleus $\approx 10^{-15}\ \text{m}$ (the ratio $\approx 10^5$).

## 3. Closest approach

A head-on $\alpha$ particle stops momentarily where all its kinetic energy has become electrical potential energy:

$$
E_k = \frac{k(2e)(Ze)}{r_{\min}}.
$$

**Example 1.** $5\ \text{MeV}$ $\alpha$ particles on gold ($Z = 79$): $r_{\min} = \dfrac{9 \times 10^9 \cdot 2 \cdot 79 \cdot (1.6 \times 10^{-19})^2}{5 \times 1.6 \times 10^{-13}} \approx 4.5 \times 10^{-14}\ \text{m}$ — much smaller than the atom, which shows the nucleus is tiny (and an upper limit for its size).

**Example 2.** The ratio of the sizes of nucleus and atom is $10^{-15} : 10^{-10} = 10^{-5}$; if the atom were a stadium, the nucleus would be a pea.

## 4. Problems with the nuclear model

An orbiting electron would radiate and spiral into the nucleus, and the model cannot explain discrete spectra; **Bohr** (next lesson) resolved these with quantised states. Later, the **quantum model** replaced orbits by probability distributions (orbitals).

## Common mistakes

- Saying most $\alpha$ particles are deflected.
- Forgetting that $\alpha$ particles are positively charged, repelled by the nucleus.
- Mixing up the sizes of the atom and nucleus.

## Practice problems

1. Which observation shows the nucleus is much smaller than the atom?
2. Why would the plum-pudding model not give large-angle scattering?

**Answers.** (1) The vast majority of $\alpha$ particles pass straight through. (2) The positive charge is spread over the whole atom, so no strong localised force exists.
